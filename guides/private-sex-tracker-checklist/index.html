The 6-question checklist for telling a private sex tracker from a “private” one

By the team at OMS Group, makers of Petaly · Updated November 2026 · 4 min read

Almost every sex tracker says it's private. "No tracking." "Encrypted." "Your data, your control." The words are free, and by now every developer has learned to use them, including some whose own privacy label, filed with the app store, quietly contradicts the marketing copy a few screens later.

So the words aren't the test. Here's the test we'd actually use, and the one we built Petaly to pass. Every check takes a couple of minutes, needs nothing but your phone, and works on any intimacy tracker, ours included.

The six questions
  1. Does everything still work in airplane mode?
  2. What does the store's data-safety label say?
  3. Is an account required, ever, for anything?
  4. Does deleting an entry actually delete it?
  5. What is the app asking your phone for?
  6. Could you leave today and take everything with you?

1. Turn on airplane mode. Does everything still work?

Not "can I still log something" but everything: opening the app, adding an entry, viewing your history, seeing your stats. If any of those breaks with no signal, the app needs a server for something, whatever its listing says about "offline." This is the fastest test on the list, and you can run it right now on the app you already have installed.

2. What does the store's own data-safety label say?

Every app on Google Play carries a data-safety section the developer files with the store. It is separate from, and sometimes at odds with, the app's own description. It is the one part of a listing a developer can't fully spin: it's a structured disclosure, not marketing copy, and it uses the same format for every app, so you can compare two trackers side by side in a minute each.

Look for three things: whether data is collected, whether it's shared with third parties, and whether it's encrypted in transit. That last one only matters if something is being transmitted in the first place. Make this the first thing you read on any app in this category, before the screenshots and before the reviews.

"Nothing leaves your phone" and "everything is encrypted before it leaves your phone" are two very different privacy postures. Only one of them means what most people assume "private" means.

3. Is an account required for anything?

Not just for backup or sync, but for the app to work at all, or for premium features. An account is a login your data is tied to on someone else's server, by definition. "No account" isn't only a convenience. It removes a whole category of risk, because there is no server-side identity for your entries to be attached to.

4. If you delete an entry, is it actually gone?

An app that keeps your data on a server can't always guarantee deletion the way a plain on-device file can. The entry might survive in a backup, a sync log or an analytics event even after it disappears from your screen. If an app's own data-safety section says data "can't be deleted," that isn't a bug being confessed. It's a fact about where your information lives, and it's worth knowing before you log anything you'd want to be able to erase later.

One honest footnote: if you've switched on Android's own device backup, a copy of an app's data can also sit in your Google account's backup. That copy is yours and under your control, but it's one more place an entry can live.

5. What is the app asking your phone for?

Open Settings → Apps → the app → Permissions. A tracker has no reason to want your contacts, your microphone, your location or your files. Anything on that list should have an obvious reason to be there.

Network access won't appear in that list, because Android treats it as a routine permission. That's why questions 1 and 2 matter more than this one, and why this one works best as a quick sanity check on top of them.

6. Could you leave today, taking everything with you?

A private app shouldn't have to earn your loyalty by making your own history hard to extract. Look for a plain export: a file you can save yourself, keep as a backup you control, or import somewhere else. If the only way to keep your history is to keep paying or keep the app installed forever, that's a retention mechanism wearing a privacy label.

Why these six, and not a longer list

Each question can be answered in a couple of minutes, using only your phone and the app's public store listing. That's deliberate. A privacy checklist that takes an hour to run is a checklist nobody runs.
